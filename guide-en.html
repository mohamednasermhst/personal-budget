1. The big picture

Most budgeting tools tell you what you spent. This one tells you what is coming.

You enter your income and your commitments once. From then on the dashboard projects twelve pay periods ahead and shows, for each one, whether the money coming in covers the money going out — and how much is genuinely safe to set aside.

How the pieces connect

Everything flows in one direction:

Income Ledger and Expense Ledger (plus P2P Lending) are what you enter. They are the only places anything is typed.

Those entries feed the calculation engine, which works out each period's total cash, what is still due, what remains, and what can safely be saved.

The results appear in three read-only views: Monthly Cash Flow (this period in detail), The Year at a Glance (the shape of the year), and Monthly Summary (period-by-period figures).

That is why the dashboard is ordered the way it is: Where you stand first, What's driving it below. You check the top; you edit the bottom.

2. The one idea that explains everything

The dashboard does not think in calendar months. It thinks in pay periods.

If your salary arrives on the 24th, your period runs from the 24th of one month to the 23rd of the next. That is the money you actually have to work with, and the window in which your bills have to fit.

This is set once, at the top of Monthly Cash Flow: Pay period starts on day.

Why this matters more than it sounds

A bill due on the 28th and a bill due on the 2nd feel like they are in different months. They are not — if you are paid on the 24th, both are paid out of the same salary. Calendar thinking hides that; pay-period thinking makes it obvious.

3. Monthly Cash Flow

The top section. It shows one pay period in full detail.

FigureWhat it means
Total IncomeMoney that has arrived in this period, up to your viewing date.
Total ExpenseWhat is still due before the period ends. Already-paid items are excluded — they have left your account.
Total CashWhat you have right now: money carried in, plus income received, minus what has already gone out.
Remaining CashTotal Cash minus what is still due. This is the number that matters. If it is negative, you are short.
Amount to SaveWhat you can set aside without creating a shortfall in any later period. See below.

Amount to Save — the clever bit

This is not simply "whatever is left over". Before suggesting a figure, the dashboard looks ahead at every remaining period. If November has a large school fee that would leave you short, it will not tell you to save that money in September.

In other words: it only calls money "safe to save" if saving it does not create a problem later.

Viewing as of

A date picker that lets you look at the dashboard as if it were a different day — travel forward to see how October looks, or back to check what happened. Everything updates: which period you are in, what has been paid, what is still ahead.

It resets to today whenever you reopen the app. That is deliberate — you should never open the dashboard weeks later and be silently reading a stale date.

Cash on hand — a reconciliation check

Optional. Tick it, enter your actual bank balance and the date you checked it. The dashboard compares that against what your entries predict.

If they differ, it offers to record the difference as a new ledger entry, so your projections start from a true figure rather than a theoretical one. It can keep this corrected automatically, and it logs every correction so nothing changes without a record.

4. The Year at a Glance

A twelve-period chart. Bars are expenses, the line is your projected savings balance, and a dot marks any period that runs short.

Tap any bar for a full breakdown of that period: income received, income still expected, expenses already paid, and expenses still due — each itemised.

Note on the bars: they always show each period's full commitment, not just what is still unpaid. A month whose bills have all been paid has not become a cheap month, and showing it as one would be misleading. The paid/unpaid split lives in the breakdown below the chart.

5. Monthly Summary

The same twelve periods as a table. Useful when you want exact figures rather than shapes.

Two columns deserve explanation:

  • Leftover — cash carried in from the previous period. If a period ends short, the shortfall carries forward rather than quietly disappearing.
  • Saving Account Balance — the running total of everything set aside, less any ad-hoc spending.

6. Income Ledger

Every source of money coming in. Three types:

TypeUse it for
SalaryRecurring pay. Set the day of the month. You can add future raises — the dashboard applies each from the month you specify, so a raise in March shows up in March and not before.
One-offA bonus, a transfer, a refund. A single amount on a single date.
RentalTenant income paid by cheques. Enter the contract dates, the total, and how many cheques — the dashboard spreads them across the contract automatically.

7. Expense Ledger

Everything going out. Grouped by category so a long list stays navigable.

Frequency

  • Monthly — same amount, same day, every month.
  • Yearly — once a year on a set date, like school fees or insurance.
  • One-off — a single dated expense that will not repeat.
  • Custom — you list the exact dates. Right for instalment plans that end after a set number of payments.

Overrides

For a monthly expense that varies in one particular month. Your household spend might be 7,000 normally but 6,000 in August — an override changes that single month without disturbing the pattern.

Categories and their behaviour

Categories are not just labels. Under Manage categories you can give one a behaviour:

  • Statement billing — for credit cards. See the next section; this one changes the maths.
  • Deficit-cut suggestion — when a period runs short, the dashboard suggests trimming these first. Use it for discretionary categories, not for a car loan you cannot skip.
  • Finite term — has an end date, then stops.

Duplicate

Copies a row with everything intact — amount, dates, category, overrides. Faster than re-entering a near-identical expense.

8. Credit cards — how they really work here

This is the part most budgeting tools get wrong, and it is worth understanding.

You do not pay a card transaction individually. It lands on a statement, and the whole statement is settled in one payment. So a card transaction has two relevant dates:

  • Statement day — decides which statement a transaction appears on.
  • Payment day — when you actually settle that statement. This is when the money leaves you.

A worked example

Statement day 6, payment day 24, salary on the 24th.

You buy something on 20 August. That is after the 6 August statement, so it lands on the 6 September statement. You settle that statement on 24 September.

So the dashboard charges it to the period beginning 24 September — not to the one you were in when you bought it.

This is the correct answer: that purchase does not touch your August salary at all.

The practical consequence: the payment day decides which period a card charge falls into. Set it to 24 and charges land in the next period; set it earlier than your pay day and they land in the current one.

The individual transaction dates only decide which statement each one belongs to. You never pay them separately, and the dashboard never pretends you do.

9. P2P Lending

Informal loans between you and another person — money you have lent out, or borrowed. Both directions flow into the forecast.

DirectionWhat happens in the forecast
BorrowedThe money arrives as income on the loan date. Repayments become expenses.
LentThe money leaves as an expense on the loan date. Repayments come back as income.

Three principles worth knowing

  • Only payments you actually record reduce a balance. Nothing is assumed settled because a date passed. An overdue loan still shows its full balance — because it is still owed.
  • Partial payments reduce the balance without changing the agreed plan. The forecast then projects only what remains.
  • Open-ended loans ("pay me back when you can") are supported, but deliberately stay out of the forecast. With no agreed date there is nothing reliable to project, and inventing one would put money in your projections that may never arrive. The balance still counts in the totals.

Interest and currency

Interest can be a flat agreed amount ("borrow 10,000, repay 11,000") or an annual rate that compounds. A loan in another currency is tracked but stays out of the forecast until you supply an exchange rate — converting at a guessed rate would put an unreliable figure into every total.

10. Ad-hoc Savings Spending

One-off purchases paid from savings rather than regular income. Each entry reduces your Saving Account Balance from its date onward, and leaves your income, expenses and Amount to Save untouched.

Use it when you dip into what you have put aside — so the savings figure stays honest.

11. Your data, backups and privacy

Your figures never leave your device. There is no account and no server. Everything lives in your browser's storage on your own phone, reachable only by this app.

Passphrase protection

Optional. Turn it on and your data is encrypted on the device — the key comes from your passphrase and is never stored anywhere. Without it, the data cannot be read by anyone, including this app.

If you forget the passphrase, the data cannot be recovered. Not by us, not by anyone. That is what makes the encryption real — a recovery route would be a second way in. Export a backup before you turn it on.

Backups — please do this

Browser storage can be cleared: by you, by the browser reclaiming space, or on a phone left unused for a week or so. If that happens the app still works, but it opens empty.

Export backup gives you a block of text. Save it in your notes or email it to yourself. Import backup restores everything from it in one paste.

The dashboard prompts you when your data has changed since the last backup, and again if it has been a while. Those prompts are worth heeding — a backup is the only thing that can bring your figures back.

Two devices, two sets of data

Data lives per device. Your phone and your computer keep separate copies, and two people each keep their own. Export and import is how you move figures between them.

12. Everyday workflows

Setting up for the first time

  1. Set Pay period starts on day to the day your salary arrives.
  2. Add your salary in the Income Ledger.
  3. Add your recurring commitments — rent, loans, utilities, school fees.
  4. If you use a credit card, set its statement and payment days under Manage categories.
  5. Tick cash on hand, enter your real balance, and accept the adjustment it suggests.
  6. Export a backup.

The monthly check

  1. Open the app. Look at Remaining Cash — negative means you are short.
  2. Glance at the chart for the months ahead. A gold bar means expenses exceed income that period.
  3. If a period is short, the dashboard names which categories to trim and by how much.
  4. Update your cash on hand if the figure has drifted.

When something changes

Add or edit the entry, then export a fresh backup. The prompt will remind you.
